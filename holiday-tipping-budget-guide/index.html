How to Build a Holiday Tipping List and Budget for the People Who Help You All Year

Holiday gifting extras guide · DeskLark Studio

The people who help all year -- a hairdresser, a mail carrier, a childcare provider, a dog walker, a building super -- rarely get their own line in a holiday budget, which means each one gets decided individually, in December, out of whatever's left after gifts and hosting. A short list built once, with a clear sense of what each entry is for, turns a dozen scattered last-minute decisions into one planned category.

Why this category is the easiest one to forget until it's urgent

Gifts for family have an obvious deadline and an obvious list. Holiday tipping doesn't -- there's no single reminder that it's time, no formal list anyone hands you, and the people on it are easy to overlook individually even though the group of them, taken together, made the rest of the year noticeably easier. That combination -- real but easy to forget, no natural deadline -- is exactly why it tends to get handled purchase by purchase in December instead of planned like the rest of the holiday budget.

Build the list once, separate from deciding amounts

The useful first step is just naming everyone who might belong on the list, without yet deciding what to do for any of them: regular service providers (hair, nails, cleaning, lawn care), people who show up consistently in a caregiving role (childcare, tutoring, pet care), people tied to where you live (building staff, a mail carrier, a regular delivery driver), and anyone else who comes to mind once you start thinking about who made this year easier. Getting the full list down first, the same way a Christmas gift list works best built completely before assigning any amounts, keeps the list from growing piecemeal through December as you keep remembering one more person.

Not everyone on the list needs the same kind of gesture

A cash tip, a small gift, or a handwritten card are all legitimate ways to close out this list, and which one fits depends on the relationship, not a fixed rule that applies to everyone equally. Some service relationships have a clear cash-tip convention behind them; others are better suited to a small gift or a genuine note of thanks, especially where a cash tip could actually feel awkward or against a workplace's own policy. Deciding gift-versus-cash-versus-card per person, rather than picking one format and applying it to the whole list, tends to land better with each individual relationship.

Building the list in one sitting

Regular service providers · caregiving or childcare-adjacent helpers · people tied to where you live · anyone else who made a specific part of this year easier. For each name, decide the gesture (cash, small gift, or card) before deciding any amount -- separating "who and how" from "how much" makes the list itself much faster to finish, and the amount decision easier once every name and gesture is already settled.

Checking whether cash is even appropriate before assuming it is

Some workplaces and some individual providers have their own policy about accepting cash tips or gifts from clients -- worth a quick, low-pressure check ("is there anything you're not able to accept?") rather than assuming the same convention applies everywhere. This is a genuinely person-by-person and workplace-by-workplace question, not something this guide can answer in general, which is exactly why it belongs on the checklist rather than being assumed away.

Funding the list ahead of December, the same way as any other holiday cost

Because most of the people on this list are known well before the holidays -- you already know your hairdresser, your mail carrier, your childcare provider -- the tipping list is a good candidate for its own small sinking fund, built up gradually starting in the fall rather than pulled from whatever cash is on hand during the busiest spending month of the year. Treating it as its own funded category inside the wider holiday budget, rather than an overflow expense, is what keeps it from quietly competing with the gift budget in December.

Reusing the list next year instead of rebuilding it

Once built, the list itself barely changes year to year -- mostly the same people, occasionally a new addition or a removal. Keeping a simple running version from one December to the next turns what felt like a scramble the first year into a five-minute update in every year after.
