Do Freelancers Have to Pay Quarterly Estimated Taxes?

Freelancer taxes & cash flow guide · DeskLark Studio

This is one of the first questions almost every new freelancer runs into, and the honest answer is "it depends on your specific numbers" -- which isn't a satisfying answer, but it's the accurate one. This guide walks through what it actually depends on and where to get the current, exact rules, without inventing a rate, threshold, or due date that might be wrong by the time you read it.

Not Tax Advice

This article is general information only, not tax advice, and doesn't state any specific tax rate, dollar threshold, or due date -- those change and depend on your full situation. Confirm current rules at IRS.gov (or your country's tax authority) and with a licensed tax professional.

Why there's no single yes-or-no answer

Whether you owe quarterly estimated taxes at all -- and if so, how much -- depends on things that are different for every freelancer: your total income from all sources (freelance and otherwise), your filing status, your deductions, whether you also have a W-2 job with tax already withheld from it, and what you owed the prior year. Two freelancers with identical freelance income can have completely different quarterly-tax situations because everything else about their financial picture differs. That's exactly why this guide won't hand you a number -- any single figure would be wrong for a large share of the people reading it.

Questions that point you toward an answer

  • Is freelancing your only income, or do you also have a job with taxes withheld? If a W-2 job is already withholding tax on your behalf, that withholding may cover some or all of what you'd otherwise owe on freelance income, changing whether -- and how much -- you need to pay quarterly on top of it.
  • Is this your first year freelancing, or do you have a prior year's tax return to look at? If you've filed as a freelancer before, your prior year's actual results are a concrete, personal data point a general article can't give you.
  • How much do you expect to owe when you file? Tax authorities generally set a threshold below which quarterly payments aren't required -- but the exact figure is set by the IRS (or your country's authority) and is exactly the kind of number that can change from one tax year to the next, so it needs to come from the current, official source rather than an article like this one.

Where to get the actual current answer

For US freelancers, IRS.gov publishes the current rules, forms, and worksheets for estimated taxes, updated for the current tax year. A licensed tax professional (CPA or enrolled agent) can also run your specific numbers and tell you definitively whether you need to pay quarterly, how much, and when payments are due for the year in question. If you're outside the US, your own country's tax authority is the equivalent source. None of these sources are optional extras here -- they're the only place this kind of specific, current number should come from.

If you're still not sure

A reasonable default for a brand-new freelancer who genuinely doesn't know yet: set aside a portion of every payment (using your own researched percentage, not a guessed one) in a dedicated account as if you might owe quarterly, and confirm with a tax professional before your first filing year closes whether you actually needed to and whether your set-aside is in the right range. Having the money already set aside costs you nothing if it turns out you didn't need to pay quarterly -- it becomes savings instead. Not having it set aside, and discovering you did need to, is a much harder position to be in.

State and local taxes are a separate question

Everything above concerns federal estimated taxes. Depending on where you live and work, state and sometimes local tax authorities may have their own, entirely separate quarterly-payment rules -- different thresholds, different due dates, and in some places no state income tax requirement at all. A "no" answer at the federal level doesn't automatically mean "no" at the state level, and vice versa. This is worth asking a tax professional about specifically, since state rules vary enough that a federal-focused article like this one shouldn't attempt to summarize fifty different sets of state requirements.

What if your situation changes partway through the year

A freelancer's income rarely stays flat all year -- a slow first quarter followed by a busy second half, or the reverse, is common. If your income picture changes significantly partway through the year, it's worth revisiting the quarterly-tax question again rather than assuming January's answer still holds in September. A tax professional can help you adjust your estimated payments as the year unfolds, which is generally more manageable than discovering a large gap only when you file.

The cost of guessing wrong runs in both directions

Underestimating what you owe and paying too little during the year can mean a larger bill (and potentially a penalty, depending on the specifics) when you file. But overestimating and setting aside more than you'll actually owe isn't free either -- it's money sitting unavailable for other uses over the course of a year, even though you eventually get it back as a refund or credit. Neither direction is disastrous if caught and corrected, which is exactly why checking in with a tax professional periodically, rather than only at filing time, tends to serve freelancers better than a "set it and forget it" approach based on a first-year guess.

What to do once you know your answer

If it turns out you do need to pay quarterly, the next practical problem is purely mechanical: tracking income as it comes in, applying your own researched set-aside percentage consistently, and having the right amount accumulated by each due date -- whatever that date turns out to be for the current tax year, per your own research. That's a bookkeeping problem, not a tax-law problem, and it's the part a calculator can actually help with once the tax-law questions above are settled with a real, current source.
